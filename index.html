For kids

How do birds fly together without a boss?

Have you seen a big group of birds swoop across the sky and turn all at once? It looks like someone is giving orders. Nobody is! Each bird only watches the few birds right next to it, and follows three tiny rules.

1

Don't bump

If a friend is too close, scoot away a little.

Separation
2

Go the same way

Fly in the direction your friends are flying.

Alignment
3

Stay together

Don't wander off alone. Move toward your friends.

Cohesion

That's all. When every bird does this, the whole flock moves like one giant animal. In 1986 a computer scientist named Craig Reynolds made pretend birds on a computer that follow these rules. He called them "boids".

Try it

In the game above, slide Cohesion to 0 and watch everyone drift apart. Slide Separation to 0 and they all squash into one blob. Or try it with friends at the playground: everyone walks around following the three rules, with no leader, and you'll make your own flock.

In technology

Where boids is used in the real world

Boids started in film and games, and that is still where it is used most. The idea spread wherever many agents must coordinate without a central controller, each reacting only to its neighbours, moment by moment.

Where it started

Movies

Batman Returns (1992) used boids for its bat swarms and penguin armies. Reynolds received a Scientific and Technical Academy Award in 1998 for this line of work. Flocks, schools and crowd scenes in many films since use the same steering ideas.

Video games

Birds, fish and crowds in game worlds, and groups of units that move together without bumping. Reynolds' steering behaviours (seek, flee, follow a path, avoid obstacles) are a standard part of game AI.

Machines that move

Drones and robots

In 2018 researchers in Budapest flew about 30 drones outdoors as a true flock, each using only local rules. Swarm robotics (for example Harvard's 1,000-robot Kilobots) builds on the same idea.

Most drone light shows are not boids: each drone follows a path planned in advance by a central computer.

Traffic

Adaptive cruise control and truck platooning apply two of the rules directly: keep a safe gap (separation) and match the speed of the vehicle ahead (alignment).

Science and safety

Biology

Researchers use boids-style models to study real starling murmurations, fish schools and locust swarms, testing which simple rules reproduce the shapes they observe.

Crowd safety

Evacuation simulators for stadiums, airports and metro stations use agent models from the same family, so designers can test exits before anything is built.

Software, data and AI

Data engineering

Data platforms that share work without a master: consistent hashing in Dynamo-style stores (each node owns a slice of the key ring and hands keys to its neighbours when nodes join or leave), retries with jitter (pipelines wait a slightly random time before retrying a recovered API, so they don't all hit it at once), and data mesh (domain teams own their data products, kept compatible by shared data contracts rather than a central team).

AI agents

Multi-agent systems are the honest match. In Stanford's Generative Agents study (2023), one AI agent was told to host a party; invitations spread agent to agent and guests showed up, without anyone scripting it. Agents sharing work without an orchestrator show the boids failure modes directly: duplicated tasks, clashing conventions, drift from the goal.

The lesson for testing AI. You cannot read three rule weights and predict the flock; you have to run it. The same holds for AI agents: you can't read the prompts and predict the behaviour, so you run evals, many times, because results vary run to run, and you re-run the whole suite after any change.

For teams

What a flock teaches a team

A great team, like a flock, doesn't need someone steering every move. It needs a few rules that everyone follows, and enough visibility to react to the people around them. Use the presets in the game to see each failure mode.

Flock ruleIn a teamWhen it's missing
SeparationClear ownership. Know what's yours and what's someone else's.Two people fix the same pipeline. Groupthink and crowding.
AlignmentShared direction: standards, data contracts, a common definition of done.Lone wolves: good work in different directions that doesn't add up.
CohesionStay connected. Share progress, ask for help, help others.People drift off and work alone; the team stops being a team.
Vision radiusHow far people look: across squads, not just their own.Silos. Data, platform, QA and BI each form their own little flock.

Good rules beat micromanagement

A flock has no one shouting directions. Clear shared rules mean fewer decisions need escalating.

Balance matters

All "stay close" and no space gives groupthink. All space and no "stay close" gives silos.

Widen your view

Birds that only watch their nearest two neighbours form small separate groups. So do teams.

A few people can steer the group

Research on animal groups (Couzin, 2005) found that a small share of informed members can guide a whole group. Share context and others follow.

Great teams aren't built by controlling everyone. They're built by agreeing on a few simple rules and everyone following them.

A caution: this is a metaphor. People plan, remember and talk to each other; birds don't. Use it to start a conversation, not to model your org chart.
